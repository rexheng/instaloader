# 052 · The Minimum Wage Study That Used New Jersey as a Lab

**One idea:** to estimate what a policy did, subtract the control group's change over time from the treated group's change. The control's change is the "what would have happened anyway", and it only works if the two groups would have moved in parallel.

## Palette (colour -> one meaning, whole episode)

| Colour | Meaning |
|---|---|
| PURPLE | New Jersey: the treated group (dots, line, wage bar, its terms in equations) |
| BLUE | Pennsylvania: the control group (dots, line, wage bar, its terms in equations) |
| YELLOW | the estimated effect of the raise (the gap between real and ghost New Jersey, the estimate meter, delta) |
| RED | bias: the amount a naive estimate is off by |
| INK / GRAY / DIM | text; axes, citations, secondary labels; dimmed context |

Dashed purple = New Jersey's counterfactual ("ghost"), never observed.

## Narration (beat by beat)

**1. Cold open (0:00-0:14)** — border line, two wage bars, NJ bar grows; "jobs?" arrow under NJ.
> April first, nineteen ninety-two. New Jersey raises its minimum wage from four twenty-five to five oh five an hour. Pennsylvania, next door, stays at four twenty-five. Textbook economics says New Jersey's fast-food restaurants should now cut jobs. Did they? Make your guess now. [pause 1.6]

**2. Stakes / the data (0:14-0:30)** — 410 restaurant dots fill both sides; citation.
> You can't randomly assign a minimum wage to a state. So two economists, David Card and Alan Krueger, phoned four hundred and ten fast-food restaurants on both sides of the border, once in February, and again in November.

**3. Unit + chart (0:30-0:40)** — dots collapse into averages on the chart.
> For each store they counted workers, with a part-timer as half a worker, and averaged.

**4. Naive 1 (0:40-1:00)** — NJ points and segment; purple change bracket; estimate meter reads +0.59.
> Start with the obvious comparison. The average New Jersey store had twenty point four workers in February, and twenty-one point oh in November. Up by point five nine. So the raise added jobs.

> Except nineteen ninety-two was a recession year. This comparison credits the wage with everything else that changed between February and November.

**5. Naive 2 (1:00-1:22)** — PA November point; meter swings to -0.14; PA February point; red gap -2.89.
> So compare the two states in November instead. Pennsylvania stores averaged twenty-one point two, so New Jersey is point one four lower, and the raise cost jobs. Two obvious methods, two different signs. This one credits the wage with every way the states differed, and in February, before any raise, New Jersey's stores were already two point eight nine workers smaller.

**6. Insight (1:22-2:12)** — PA segment + blue bracket; copy slides onto NJ; dashed ghost; yellow gap; equation from brackets.
> Pennsylvania never got the raise. Its drop, two point one six workers, shows what those months did to a restaurant with no new wage. So where would New Jersey have ended up, on that same path? [pause 1.2]

> Slide Pennsylvania's drop onto New Jersey's February point. This dashed line is New Jersey without the raise, at eighteen point three. The real New Jersey is at twenty-one. That gap, two point seven five workers per store, is the estimate.

> Written out, it's New Jersey's change minus Pennsylvania's change. A difference in differences.

> It also repairs both naive answers. Plus point five nine was off by Pennsylvania's drop. Minus point one four was off by February's gap. Correct either one, and you land on the same two point seven five.

**7. Assumption / stress test (2:12-2:38)** — simulated panel with several pre-periods, diverging; ghost lands wrong.
> Everything rests on that dashed line, the assumption that the two states would have moved in parallel. After the policy, no one can see it. Before, you can check. In this simulated example the lines were already drifting apart, and the method reports an effect where the true one is zero. Card and Krueger had a single before-survey, so they couldn't run this check.

**8. Payoff (2:38-3:05)** — 2.76 ± interval on a number line, citation; debate lines with citations.
> They published two point seven six, plus or minus one point three six, in the American Economic Review, nineteen ninety-four. No sign that the higher wage cut jobs. Then came the fight. Neumark and Wascher, using restaurant payroll records, found jobs fell. Card and Krueger answered with government employment records, and found no drop.

**9. Landing (3:05-3:15)** — back to the border picture; a blue arrow carries Pennsylvania's drop across the border to NJ.
> So did New Jersey lose jobs? Looking at New Jersey alone could never tell you. The most important number in a study of New Jersey came from Pennsylvania.
