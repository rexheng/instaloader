# 069 — Two Nearly Identical Crowds: One Riots, One Doesn't

**One idea:** whether a crowd riots is decided by the whole threshold distribution (the first
point where the willing-count F(r) meets the line r), not by its average, so a one-person change
can flip the outcome and a crowd's behavior says little about who is in it.

## Palette (colour -> one meaning)
- BLUE — a person / the willing count F(r) (blocks, cells, the curve)
- RED — rioting (rioting cells, the "rioting =" counter)
- PURPLE — the line "willing = rioting" (y = r) and the r term
- YELLOW — focus: the one changed person, the staircase / equilibrium point, the spread sigma
- INK text; GRAY axes, citations, context; DIM context

## Beats (narration, spoken form)

**Cold open (0:00–0:12)** — two 10x10 grids of numbered circles, thresholds 0–99.
1. Two crowds, a hundred people each. Each number is how many others must already be rioting before that person joins in.
2. One person differs. Here, a one. Here, a two. How many people riot in each crowd? [pause 1.5]

**Stakes + naive build**
3. When a protest turns violent, we explain it by who was in the crowd. So describe each crowd the usual way, by its average threshold.
4. Left: forty nine point five. Right: forty nine point five one. By that measure they are the same crowd, so they should do the same thing.

**Run it / fail**
5. Run them. The person with threshold zero breaks a window. One rioter is enough for the one. Two rioters bring in the two, and the wave carries all the way to a hundred.
6. On the right, the same instigator breaks the same window. But nobody here has a threshold of one. The two waits for a second rioter who never comes.
7. A hundred against one. The average missed by ninety nine people, because a riot is a chain, and an average can't see a missing link.

**Insight — the picture**
8. So draw the chain. Across: how many are rioting so far. Up: how many people are willing at that count, everyone whose threshold is that low or lower.
9. The purple line is where willing equals rioting. Wherever a bar pokes above it, someone new joins, and the count steps right.
10. In the left crowd every bar sits one person above the line, so the staircase climbs and never stops.
11. Now the right crowd. One person's threshold moves from one to two, so this bar loses them. At one rioter, only one person is willing. The bar touches the line, and the riot stops dead.
12. Call the willing count F of r. The riot ends at the first r where F of r equals r.

**Payoff (Granovetter 1978, recomputed)**
13. Granovetter ran this picture on a bell curve of thresholds, average twenty five. Make the crowd more varied, with more radicals and more holdouts. Does the riot grow or shrink? [pause 1.5]
14. With a narrow spread, the curve meets the line almost at once, and barely anyone riots. Widen it, and the count creeps up to about six. Then, near a spread of twelve point two, that first crossing vanishes, and the riot leaps to nearly a hundred. The average never moved.
15. This is a stylized model. Nobody can measure a real person's threshold, so it explains no particular riot. It shows what an average hides.

**Landing**
16. Granovetter imagined the next day's headlines. Left: a crowd of radicals engaged in riotous behavior. Right: a demented troublemaker broke a window while a group of solid citizens looked on.
17. Same hundred people, minus one link. What a crowd did tells you very little about who was in it.
